# Survey research for a TradesQuest hackathon build

Papers that should shape QuestSignal, an agentic layer on TradesQuest (stonebyte.bid): game evidence becomes a hire-ready profile, an employer shortlist, and a public page that answer engines can cite. The hackathon is Musa Labs at SF Tech Week. Aigeo (theaigeo.com) is the generative-engine-optimization partner.

Scope is peer-reviewed work from 2018–2026 where it exists, checked against Crossref, OpenAlex, Europe PMC, and publisher pages in October 2026. Twenty-five papers are below. HVAC- and plumbing-specific game reviews in this window are scarce. The nearest evidence is construction-safety simulation, electrical-power game-based learning, and apprentice surveys that include electrical and plumbing.

Each entry has the full citation, a link, the year, the study type, what was studied, the finding that should change the build, and one use for QuestSignal.

## 1. Literature surveys, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses

### 1.1 Sailer and Homner (2020) — gamification meta-analysis

**Citation.** Sailer, M., & Homner, L. (2020). The gamification of learning: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review, 32*(1), 77–112. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w

**Year.** 2020 (first online 15 August 2019).

**Type.** Meta-analysis.

**What it studied.** Random-effects synthesis of gamification versus other instruction on cognitive, motivational, and behavioral learning outcomes.

**Finding that matters.** Effects are small: cognitive *g* = 0.49 (95% CI [0.30, 0.69], *k* = 19, *N* = 1,686), motivational *g* = 0.36 (95% CI [0.18, 0.54], *k* = 16, *N* = 2,246), and behavioral *g* = 0.25 (95% CI [0.04, 0.46], *k* = 9, *N* = 951). The cognitive effect held in the high-rigor subset. Motivational and behavioral effects did not. Game fiction, and competition combined with collaboration, were the moderators that moved behavioral outcomes. Which elements produce cognitive gains is still unresolved.

**QuestSignal.** Treat XP, badges, and leaderboards as engagement furniture, and put the learning claim on the task itself: a shared scenario where players collaborate inside a team and compete across teams.

### 1.2 Dahalan, Alias, and Shaharom (2024) — gamification in vocational education

**Citation.** Dahalan, F., Alias, N., & Shaharom, M. S. N. (2024). Gamification and game based learning for vocational education and training: A systematic literature review. *Education and Information Technologies, 29*(2), 1279–1317. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-022-11548-w

**Year.** 2024 (first online 12 January 2023).

**Type.** Systematic literature review (PRISMA; 17 studies).

**What it studied.** Where gamification and game-based learning show up in vocational education and training, which tools are used, and what outcomes are reported.

**Finding that matters.** The set is small and concentrated in Asian vocational schools and colleges, mostly engineering and healthcare. Digital platforms and simulation are the tools the authors call most promising. The included studies associate gamification and game-based learning with better academic performance, engagement, and motivation, and the authors say the field still lacks evidence on which strategies fit vocational learning.

**QuestSignal.** Demo a simulated trade task on a digital platform, and say the vocational evidence base is 17 studies, so the product is an application of that pattern, not a settled proof that trades gamification works.

### 1.3 Krath, Schürmann, and von Korflesch (2021) — theories behind gamification

**Citation.** Krath, J., Schürmann, L., & von Korflesch, H. F. O. (2021). Revealing the theoretical basis of gamification: A systematic review and analysis of theory in research on gamification, serious games and game-based learning. *Computers in Human Behavior, 125*, 106963. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2021.106963

**Year.** 2021.

**Type.** Systematic review.

**What it studied.** Which psychological theories papers actually use to explain gamification, serious games, and game-based learning.

**Finding that matters.** The review found 118 different theories. The field explains motivation, behavior, and learning with incompatible mechanisms, so a points system has no single agreed reason it should change skill.

**QuestSignal.** Name one mechanism on the profile — competence feedback on a trade task, or progress toward a stated apprenticeship milestone — and keep the copy consistent with that mechanism.

### 1.4 Li, Ma, and Shi (2023) — a larger pooled gamification effect

**Citation.** Li, M., Ma, S., & Shi, Y. (2023). Examining the effectiveness of gamification as a tool promoting teaching and learning in educational settings: A meta-analysis. *Frontiers in Psychology, 14*, 1253549. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1253549

**Year.** 2023.

**Type.** Meta-analysis.

**What it studied.** Forty-one studies, 49 independent samples, and more than 5,071 participants on whether gamification promotes teaching and learning.

**Finding that matters.** The pooled effect is large, *g* = 0.822 (95% CI [0.567, 1.078]), with moderator analyses. That estimate is much larger than Sailer and Homner’s. Magnitude depends on which outcomes and which studies are pooled.

**QuestSignal.** If the demo quotes an effect, quote the range (about 0.25 to 0.8 across outcomes and reviews) and name the outcome, rather than a single “gamification works this much” number.

### 1.5 Gao, González, and Yiu (2019) — construction health and safety training

**Citation.** Gao, Y., González, V. A., & Yiu, T. W. (2019). The effectiveness of traditional tools and computer-aided technologies for health and safety training in the construction sector: A systematic review. *Computers & Education, 138*, 101–115. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2019.05.003

**Year.** 2019.

**Type.** Systematic review.

**What it studied.** Whether traditional health-and-safety training and computer-aided training (serious games, simulation, virtual, augmented, and mixed reality) change knowledge, unsafe behavior, and injury rates in construction.

**Finding that matters.** Traditional tools have statistical support for those three outcomes. Computer-aided tools have some effectiveness evidence and are stronger at representing the real workplace, offering text-free interfaces, and engaging users. Evidence that games and simulation reduce injuries is still thin.

**QuestSignal.** Claim realistic, text-light practice and engagement. Leave site-injury reduction off the slide.

### 1.6 Taleb, Belayutham, and Che Ibrahim (2023) — serious games in construction

**Citation.** Taleb, M. S., Belayutham, S., & Che Ibrahim, C. K. I. (2023). Applications of serious games in construction: The current state, classifications and a proposed process framework. *International Journal of Construction Management, 23*(10), 1726–1736. https://doi.org/10.1080/15623599.2021.2006416

**Year.** 2023 (first online 2021).

**Type.** Systematic review (50 articles) plus a proposed process framework.

**What it studied.** How serious games are actually used across construction, beyond single-topic papers.

**Finding that matters.** Published construction games cluster on safety and sustainability. The review’s point is that the field still lacks a shared account of what to build and how to build it, which is why the authors propose a process framework.

**QuestSignal.** A hiring layer is an extension past the evaluated use of construction games. The demo should say the game evidence is about safe practice, and the profile is a new product on top of that evidence.

### 1.7 Man, Wen, and So (2024) — VR for construction safety

**Citation.** Man, S. S., Wen, H., & So, B. C. L. (2024). Are virtual reality applications effective for construction safety training and education? A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Journal of Safety Research, 88*, 230–243. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsr.2023.11.011

**Year.** 2024.

**Type.** Systematic review and meta-analysis.

**What it studied.** VR versus traditional methods in construction safety training and education, using 65 records from 12 articles, plus moderators.

**Finding that matters.** VR outperformed traditional methods overall (effect size 0.516, 95% CI [0.319, 0.714], *p* < 0.01). The published abstract reports standardized mean differences of 0.593 for behaviors, 0.432 for skills, and 0.777 for experience. Training context and trainees’ mean work experience were significant moderators (*p* < 0.001). The practical implication in the paper is early VR safety training for young, inexperienced workers.

**QuestSignal.** Aim the simulation evidence at early-career players, and label each exported score as behavior, skill, or experience, because those are the outcomes the meta-analysis actually separated.

### 1.8 Scorgie, Feng, Paes, Parisi, Yiu, and Lovreglio (2024) — VR safety training across industries

**Citation.** Scorgie, D., Feng, Z., Paes, D., Parisi, F., Yiu, T. W., & Lovreglio, R. (2024). Virtual reality for safety training: A systematic literature review and meta-analysis. *Safety Science, 171*, 106372. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssci.2023.106372

**Year.** 2024.

**Type.** Systematic review (52 articles, 2013–2021) and meta-analysis.

**What it studied.** VR safety training outside medicine and the military, including construction, fire, aviation, and mining, with separate analyses of knowledge acquisition and retention.

**Finding that matters.** Construction and fire dominate since 2018. Only 9.6% of studies explicitly used a theory, and only 36% measured long-term retention. Knowledge acquisition favored VR (Hedges’ *g* = 0.640, *p* < 0.001, 13 studies) with high heterogeneity (*I*² = 85.6%) and a prediction interval of about −0.86 to 2.13, so some settings still favor traditional training. Retention favored VR (*g* = 0.838, *p* < 0.001) on only eight studies.

**QuestSignal.** Say “knowledge immediately after the scenario, on average.” Retention and on-site transfer stay out of the claim, and the profile should record when the measurement was taken.

### 1.9 Chiang, Shang, and Qiao (2022) — augmented reality in vocational training

**Citation.** Chiang, F.-K., Shang, X., & Qiao, L. (2022). Augmented reality in vocational training: A systematic review of research and applications. *Computers in Human Behavior, 129*, 107125. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2021.107125

**Year.** 2022.

**Type.** Systematic review of 80 studies (2000–2021), with a meta-analysis on 17 empirical studies.

**What it studied.** Where AR is used in vocational skills training, on which devices, and whether it improves training results.

**Finding that matters.** AR shows up most in medical training, industrial maintenance, and assembly, using glasses, simulators, Unity, and panorama apps. The meta-analysis of 17 empirical studies is the authors’ basis for saying AR has a strong positive effect on vocational training results. The paper does not establish that effect for a full electrical, plumbing, or HVAC apprenticeship.

**QuestSignal.** Demo one bounded procedure — a lockout step, a joint, a startup check — because that is the grain of the AR evidence, and keep the profile tied to that procedure.

### 1.10 Giraldo, Kok, and Paterakis (2024) — games in electrical power engineering

**Citation.** Giraldo, J. S., Kok, K., & Paterakis, N. G. (2024). A structured review on game-based learning applied to electrical power and energy engineering. *Computer Applications in Engineering Education, 32*(1). https://doi.org/10.1002/cae.22686

**Year.** 2024 (first online 2023).

**Type.** Structured review (81 papers, 2002–2021).

**What it studied.** Game-based learning tools and evaluation methods in electrical power and energy systems, for both education and industry training.

**Finding that matters.** About 63% of applications were educational; serious games were common there, while virtual reality was the main training tool (56% of training papers). Only 42% of education papers and 37% of training papers analyzed learning efficacy, and more than half of those analyses were qualitative post-surveys. Industry interest is shorter induction, lower cost, and fewer accidents, which the games have not generally measured.

**QuestSignal.** A finished electrical level is a practice record. The hire-ready profile needs a separate check — a scored procedure, a rubric, or a human sign-off — because most electrical game papers never measured learning.

### 1.11 Varadarajan, Koh, and Daniel (2023) — micro-credentials for four stakeholders

**Citation.** Varadarajan, S., Koh, J. H. L., & Daniel, B. K. (2023). A systematic review of the opportunities and challenges of micro-credentials for multiple stakeholders: Learners, employers, higher education institutions and government. *International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education, 20*, 13. https://doi.org/10.1186/s41239-023-00381-x

**Year.** 2023.

**Type.** Systematic review (60 papers).

**What it studied.** How micro-credentials are defined, and what learners, employers, institutions, and governments need from them.

**Finding that matters.** Learners want short, practical, current courses. Institutions emphasize accreditation because that is what creates trust. Employers want a clear statement of the competency. Across the reviewed articles, employer worries concentrate on consistency (raised in about 80% of articles that discuss the issue), and on integrity and fraud (about 38%). There is no shared definition or size, which makes credentials hard to compare. The same literature is where Wheelahan and Moodie’s caution sits: short unbundled credentials can shift the cost of becoming employable onto the learner.

**QuestSignal.** Every public credential names the task, the evidence, the issuer, and the fact that it supplements site time. A badge image without those four fields is the product employers already distrust.

### 1.12 Yan and colleagues (2024) — LLMs in education

**Citation.** Yan, L., Sha, L., Zhao, L., Li, Y., Martinez-Maldonado, R., Chen, G., Li, X., Jin, Y., & Gašević, D. (2024). Practical and ethical challenges of large language models in education: A systematic scoping review. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 55*(1), 90–112. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13370

**Year.** 2024 (first online 2023).

**Type.** Systematic scoping review (118 peer-reviewed papers since 2017).

**What it studied.** Where large language models automate educational work, and which practical and ethical problems block use in real settings.

**Finding that matters.** The review maps 53 use cases, including question generation, feedback, and grading. The authors’ conclusion is that practicality and ethics, not raw model skill, are what stall deployment in authentic education.

**QuestSignal.** The agent may draft feedback and assemble a profile. It should log what was generated, and a person should accept the profile before it is called hire-ready.

### 1.13 Wang and colleagues (2024) — how LLM agents are built

**Citation.** Wang, L., Ma, C., Feng, X., Zhang, Z., Yang, H., Zhang, J., Chen, Z., Tang, J., Chen, X., Lin, Y., Zhao, W. X., Wei, Z., & Wen, J. (2024). A survey on large language model based autonomous agents. *Frontiers of Computer Science, 18*(6). https://doi.org/10.1007/s11704-024-40231-1

**Year.** 2024.

**Type.** Survey.

**What it studied.** A unified construction framework for LLM agents and the applications that framework covers.

**Finding that matters.** The survey organizes agents into profile, memory, planning, and action, instead of treating the model as one undifferentiated prompt. Applications diverge once those modules are specified.

**QuestSignal.** Split the demo into four visible steps: who the player is (profile), the TradesQuest evidence store (memory), the match to employer needs (planning), and the shortlist plus public page (action).

### 1.14 Zou and colleagues (2026) — human–agent collaboration

**Citation.** Zou, H. P., Huang, W.-C., Wu, Y., Guo, J., Chen, Y., Miao, C., Nguyen, H. H., Zhou, Y., Zhang, W., Fang, L., Zhang, H., Wang, F., Zhang, P., He, L., Li, Y., Li, D., Jiang, R., & Yu, P. S. (2026). LLM-based human-agent collaboration and interaction systems: A survey. *Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: ACL 2026*, 36335–36364. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2026.findings-acl.1811

**Year.** 2026.

**Type.** Survey (peer-reviewed conference findings; preprint at https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2505.00753).

**What it studied.** Why fully autonomous LLM agents fail in real tasks, and how human–agent systems add feedback or control.

**Finding that matters.** Fully autonomous agents remain unreliable because of hallucinations, weak performance on complex tasks, and safety risk. Systems that keep a human in the loop are the practical response in this survey.

**QuestSignal.** The employer shortlist is a recommendation a recruiter or mentor confirms. The demo should show the confirmation step, not an unsupervised ranking.

### 1.15 Schreieder, Schopf, and Färber (2026) — citation inside generated answers

**Citation.** Schreieder, T., Schopf, T., & Färber, M. (2026). Attribution, citation, and quotation: A survey of evidence-based text generation with large language models. *Proceedings of the 64th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Long Papers)*, 30956–31000. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2026.acl-long.1430

**Year.** 2026.

**Type.** Survey (134 papers; 300 evaluation metrics across seven dimensions).

**What it studied.** How research links LLM output to supporting evidence through attribution, citation, and quotation, and why that literature is hard to compare.

**Finding that matters.** Evidence-based generation is fragmented in terminology and metrics, and the workable idea across it is traceability: a generated sentence should point at a source a reader can check.

**QuestSignal.** Write the public page so each claim about a player has a source a model can quote — a scenario result, a rubric, a date — because answer engines cite checkable spans.

## 2. Empirical survey-research studies

These studies collected questionnaires or analyzed large trainee surveys. Sample, country, and trade mix limit how far they travel to a U.S. electrical, plumbing, or HVAC employer.

### 2.1 Findeisen, Jüttler, Neuenschwander, and Schumann (2022) — persistence after the school-to-VET transition

**Citation.** Findeisen, S., Jüttler, A., Neuenschwander, M. P., & Schumann, S. (2022). Transition from school to work – Explaining persistence intention in vocational education and training in Switzerland. *Vocations and Learning, 15*(1), 129–154. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12186-021-09282-4

**Year.** 2022.

**Type.** Empirical survey (longitudinal questionnaires; structural equation model).

**What it studied.** Two waves on 1,163 Swiss dual-VET trainees, before and after the move from school into training. Persistence intention is treated as an early marker of dropout. The authors note other work in which dropout intentions predict about 59% of actual dropouts.

**Finding that matters.** Occupational self-efficacy and perceived person–vocation fit during training both predict persistence intention. Pre-transition self-efficacy and anticipated fit work only indirectly, through those later perceptions. Social integration at the workplace predicts persistence directly and indirectly. The relationship with the trainer predicts it only indirectly. The model explains 48% of the variance in persistence intention.

**QuestSignal.** The profile should show fit, confidence on trade tasks, and whether the player is connected to a workplace or mentor. A points total does not stand in for those three.

### 2.2 Michaelis and Findeisen (2022) — fit and contract termination

**Citation.** Michaelis, C., & Findeisen, S. (2022). Influence of person-vocation fit on satisfaction and persistence in vocational training programs. *Frontiers in Psychology, 13*, 834543. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.834543

**Year.** 2022.

**Type.** Empirical survey analysis (German National Educational Panel Study).

**What it studied.** Educational match, interest congruence, and skill congruence as predictors of training satisfaction and premature contract termination for 4,097 VET trainees.

**Finding that matters.** Most mismatches do not hurt. Interest congruence and skill congruence do not change training satisfaction. Moderate overeducation is associated with higher satisfaction. Undereducation raises the probability of premature contract termination regardless of the occupation’s usual qualification level. Skill congruence does not predict termination. Interest congruence predicts lower termination only for Realistic interests, the Holland type that covers hands-on trades.

**QuestSignal.** For a trades profile, surface Realistic-task fit and prior preparation. A generic “skills match” score is a weak dropout signal in this dataset.

### 2.3 Wuttke, Heinrichs, Koegler, and Just (2024) — training quality and identification

**Citation.** Wuttke, E., Heinrichs, K., Koegler, K., & Just, A. (2024). How training quality, trainer competence, and satisfaction with training affect vocational identification of apprentices in vocational education programs. *Frontiers in Psychology, 15*, 1200279. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1200279

**Year.** 2024.

**Type.** Empirical survey (cross-sectional online questionnaire).

**What it studied.** Career identification and organizational identification among 598 apprentices in Germany and Austria in 2020–2021 (SEM on 503). About 24% were in technical apprenticeships. The sample is a convenience sample and light on first-year apprentices, who drop out more often.

**Finding that matters.** Preferred occupation predicted career identification (68% said the occupation was their preferred one). Variety of tasks predicted career identification (0.13) and organizational identification (0.08). Trainer presence predicted organizational identification (0.13) and did not predict career identification. Task complexity and autonomy did not. Explained variance was *R*² = 0.24 for career identification and *R*² = 0.68 for organizational identification. Trainer aptitude showed up through training satisfaction.

**QuestSignal.** Let the profile show task variety, a named mentor, and whether this trade is the player’s preferred path. Those are the survey predictors of staying identified with the occupation.

### 2.4 Powers (2020) — electrical, plumbing, carpentry, and bricklaying apprentices

**Citation.** Powers, T. E. (2020). *Factors lifting apprenticeship retention expectations (FLARe)* (Doctoral dissertation, Monash University). https://doi.org/10.26180/13181168.v1

**Year.** 2020.

**Type.** Empirical survey (doctoral thesis, not a journal article). Four questionnaire waves over two years.

**What it studied.** Planned persistence among 2,387 Australian apprentices in carpentry, bricklaying, electrical, and plumbing, linked to workplace and trade-school experiences.

**Finding that matters.** Workplace interest (positive) and anxiety (negative) were the strongest predictors of planned persistence. Interest fell and anxiety rose as training went on. Career indecision, worksite training, and workload predicted that slide.

**QuestSignal.** This is the closest questionnaire evidence to electrical and plumbing. The agent should watch interest and anxiety across sessions, and the demo should label the source as an Australian doctoral survey of four trades.

### 2.5 Sands and Leathem (2026) — construction employers on micro-credentials

**Citation.** Sands, K. S., & Leathem, T. (2026). The construction industry’s value perception of micro-credentials. *International Journal of Construction Education and Research*, 1–22. https://doi.org/10.1080/15578771.2026.2616673

**Year.** 2026.

**Type.** Empirical survey.

**What it studied.** Familiarity, usefulness, hiring, and advancement judgments from 95 construction professionals (executives, project managers, and other site roles).

**Finding that matters.** Most respondents had not heard of micro-credentials before the survey. They still saw a use for closing skill gaps, supporting growth, and validating a targeted competency. They did not treat micro-credentials as a replacement for traditional education or hands-on experience. Respondents who had completed one rated them more favorably. Accreditation by a recognized institution raised credibility. Employers would rather use them as a supplement for apprentices, vocational learners, and career changers.

**QuestSignal.** Address the public page to apprentices and career changers, name a recognized issuer, and state in the first screen that the record supplements site experience.

### 2.6 Perkins and Pryor (2021) — employers and digital badges

**Citation.** Perkins, J., & Pryor, M. (2021). Digital badges: Pinning down employer challenges. *Journal of Teaching and Learning for Graduate Employability, 12*(1), 24–38. https://doi.org/10.21153/jtlge2021vol12no1art1027

**Year.** 2021.

**Type.** Empirical survey (mixed methods; about 700 employers invited, 73 responded), fielded before and during COVID-19.

**What it studied.** Whether employers know about digital badges, accept them, and use them in recruiting.

**Finding that matters.** Ninety-seven percent of respondents were unfamiliar with digital badges. The authors did not find broad rejection. Employers asked for a clearer account of value, credibility, and security, and the paper’s recommendation is a tighter partnership between issuers and employers.

**QuestSignal.** The public page has to explain the credential in plain language: what was assessed, who issued it, and how a third party can verify it. Recognition will not be waiting on the employer side.

### 2.7 Kahle-Piasecki, Bereza, and Cumberland (2024) — a small employer pilot on badges

**Citation.** Kahle-Piasecki, L., Bereza, M., & Cumberland, D. M. (2024). Digital badges: A pilot study of employer perceptions. *Small Business Institute Journal, 20*(2). https://doi.org/10.53703/001c.125642

**Year.** 2024 (survey fielded in early 2020).

**Type.** Empirical survey (pilot, *N* = 15).

**What it studied.** HR, performance, and talent specialists in Northwest Ohio manufacturing, healthcare, communications, and technology firms, on whether badges count in hiring and performance review.

**Finding that matters.** Nine of 15 (60%) had earned a badge and the same share called badges a legitimate credential when judging applicants. Seven of 15 (47%) had used badges to gain workplace knowledge or credentials. A majority expected badges to matter in future hiring. The sample is too small, and too far from construction trades, to estimate current hiring practice.

**QuestSignal.** Use this only as a sign that some employers expect badges to matter later. Do not put “employers hire on badges today” in the demo.

## 3. Empirical studies that are not questionnaire surveys

Skills-based hiring and generative engine optimization are mostly vacancy studies, content experiments, and observational citation analyses. They are included because those two hackathon topics have little questionnaire research, and they are labeled by method so they are not mistaken for surveys.

### 3.1 Bone, González Ehlinger, and Stephany (2025) — skills versus degrees in job ads

**Citation.** Bone, M., González Ehlinger, E., & Stephany, F. (2025). Skills or degree? The rise of skill-based hiring for AI and green jobs. *Technological Forecasting and Social Change, 214*, 124042. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.techfore.2025.124042

**Year.** 2025.

**Type.** Empirical vacancy study (about 11 million UK online job ads, 2018 to mid-2024). Not a questionnaire.

**What it studied.** Whether employers in shortage occupations advertise skills instead of degrees, and what wage premiums those signals carry.

**Finding that matters.** From 2018 to 2023, AI roles grew by 21% as a share of postings, while mentions of university requirements in AI ads fell by 15%. AI skills carry a 23% wage premium, above the premium for degrees short of a PhD (33%). Degree wage premiums are also lower in green roles. The authors explicitly point employers toward apprenticeships, on-the-job training, vocational education, and micro-certificates when degrees cannot fill the shortage. The data are UK AI and green jobs, not U.S. skilled-trades hiring.

**QuestSignal.** Lead the shortlist with demonstrated tasks when the pitch is a shortage role. Do not claim a 23% wage premium, or a degree-free hiring norm, for electrical, plumbing, or HVAC.

### 3.2 Aggarwal and colleagues (2024) — generative engine optimization

**Citation.** Aggarwal, P., Murahari, V., Rajpurohit, T., Kalyan, A., Narasimhan, K., & Deshpande, A. (2024). GEO: Generative engine optimization. In *Proceedings of the 30th ACM SIGKDD Conference on Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining* (pp. 5–16). https://doi.org/10.1145/3637528.3671900

**Year.** 2024.

**Type.** Empirical experiment on a benchmark and on Perplexity.ai. Not a survey. Preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735

**What it studied.** How to rewrite a source page so a generative answer engine cites it. Visibility is position-adjusted word count and a subjective impression score, on GEO-bench (10,000 queries) and on one live engine.

**Finding that matters.** Adding citations, quotations from credible sources, and statistics improved position-adjusted word count by about 30–40% relative to an unoptimized page. The best single methods improved that metric by up to 41% and subjective impression by up to 28%. On Perplexity.ai the gain reached 37%. Fluency and easier wording helped about 15–30%. Keyword stuffing, the classic SEO move, did not. An authoritative tone did not meaningfully help. Which tactic wins depends on the query domain. The engine, prompts, and page corpus are the authors’ benchmark, not stonebyte.bid.

**QuestSignal.** The public page should be fluent and should include real statistics, short quotations, and citations a model can lift. It should not be keyword-stuffed, and it should not imitate an authoritative tone without sources. The 30–40% figure is a benchmark result, not a promised lift for this domain.

### 3.3 Moore and Dunne (2026) — what production engines actually cite

**Citation.** Moore, B., & Dunne, L. (2026). *What drives citations in production large language models? An observational multi-method study of two million AI citations across ten thousand web pages* (arXiv:2609.35077). https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2609.35077

**Year.** 2026 (preprint dated 12 May 2026). Not yet a journal article. Both authors are affiliated with Discovered Labs, an answer-engine benchmarking company, so this is vendor research with a published method, not an independent replication.

**Type.** Observational study. Not a questionnaire.

**What it studied.** About 2 million citations from ChatGPT, Claude, Google AI Overviews, and Gemini over six months, joined to 10,042 crawled pages from 19 B2B SaaS workspaces. More than 60 page features were tested with domain fixed effects and a nine-method consensus check. The pages are software-marketing pages, not trade-training pages.

**Finding that matters.** Three results survived the authors’ checks. Prompt–content alignment is the dominant page-level predictor (β = +0.37, 95% CI [+0.33, +0.41] on log citation count). FAQ blocks, structured data, and Core Web Vitals look helpful in pooled data and shrink to nothing, or reverse, once domain is held constant. Domain-level authority outweighs the strongest non-alignment page feature by about six times in mean absolute SHAP. Cited passages also sit toward the top third of the page (median depth 0.36).

**QuestSignal.** Write the page in the words employers use when they ask for an apprentice, and put the evidence high on the page. Schema, FAQ blocks, and page speed are weak levers here. A new domain will not out-cite established training bodies on structure alone.

## Implications for the build

### Five claims the demo can defend

1. **Simulated and game-based practice can raise knowledge, and sometimes behavior and experience, relative to traditional instruction, especially for novices.** Man, Wen, and So find VR ahead of traditional construction-safety training on behavior, skills, and experience, with larger gains for less experienced workers. Scorgie and colleagues find a medium-to-large average gain in knowledge (Hedges’ *g* = 0.64) that is heterogeneous. Dahalan and colleagues find vocational studies clustering on simulation and digital platforms. The honest sentence is “practice in a realistic scenario improves what a novice can show immediately afterward.”

2. **The stable learning effect of gamification is small, and it is cognitive.** Sailer and Homner’s cognitive effect (*g* = 0.49) survived a high-rigor cut. Their motivational and behavioral effects did not. Li, Ma, and Shi’s larger pooled estimate (*g* = 0.82) shows that the number moves with the outcome and the study set. Game fiction and competition-with-collaboration are the design features tied to behavioral gains. Points alone are not the finding.

3. **Apprentices stay when the trade fits, the work is interesting, a mentor is present, and preparation is adequate.** In Swiss dual VET, fit, occupational self-efficacy, and workplace integration explain persistence intention (Findeisen and colleagues; 48% of variance). In the German panel, undereducation and a Realistic-interest mismatch are the fit signals tied to leaving, while a generic skill mismatch is not (Michaelis and Findeisen). Among Australian electrical, plumbing, carpentry, and bricklaying apprentices, workplace interest and anxiety dominate planned persistence (Powers). In Germany and Austria, task variety, trainer presence, and a preferred occupation predict identification (Wuttke and colleagues).

4. **A skills record is hire-relevant only as a supplement, with the competency, the issuer, and a verification path in view.** Construction professionals (*n* = 95) see micro-credentials as useful for apprentices and career changers and weak as a substitute for site experience, and accreditation changes credibility (Sands and Leathem). UK vacancy data show shortage employers advertising skills and paying for them (Bone and colleagues), in AI and green jobs, not in U.S. trades. Badge surveys show employers are mostly unfamiliar (97% in Perkins and Pryor) and, in a 15-person pilot, hopeful about the future rather than changed in current practice (Kahle-Piasecki and colleagues).

5. **An agent can assemble a citable page if a human confirms it and the page is specific, sourced, and aligned to real questions.** Wang and colleagues separate profile, memory, planning, and action. Zou and colleagues, and Yan and colleagues, are the reason the last step needs a person: autonomous models hallucinate and education deployments stall on ethics and practicality. Aggarwal and colleagues show citations, quotations, statistics, and fluent wording move benchmark visibility, while keyword stuffing and an empty authoritative tone do not. Moore and Dunne show that, on production engines, language aligned to the questions and domain authority dominate FAQ schema and speed, on a B2B sample with a vendor affiliation.

### Claims the evidence does not support

- XP, a badge, or a finished level is journeyman skill, a license, or proof someone is safe on a job. Electrical game papers mostly never measured learning efficacy (Giraldo and colleagues), and construction games were evaluated for safety practice, not hiring (Taleb and colleagues).
- Game-based or VR training reduces injuries. Gao and colleagues find that injury evidence is the stronger result for traditional construction training, and computer-aided evidence is still thin.
- Gamification reliably makes people more motivated. That effect did not hold in Sailer and Homner’s high-rigor subset, and Li and colleagues’ large pooled number is a different estimate, not a license to promise motivation.
- VR or simulation always beats the classroom. Scorgie and colleagues’ prediction interval for knowledge acquisition crosses zero.
- A full HVAC, plumbing, or electrical apprenticeship has been shown to improve inside a game. The AR review is about maintenance and assembly tasks (Chiang and colleagues). Plumbing and HVAC game reviews of journal quality were not in this set.
- Micro-credentials replace an apprenticeship, a degree, or time on site. Sands and Leathem’s construction sample says the opposite, and Varadarajan and colleagues document employer distrust on consistency and fraud.
- Employers already hire tradespeople on digital badges. Perkins and Pryor’s respondents mostly did not know what a badge was.
- Skills-based hiring in AI and green UK vacancies is the skilled-trades market. Bone and colleagues recommend apprenticeships as a response to shortages. They do not measure U.S. contractor hiring.
- The agent can rank candidates fairly without a human review, an evidence log, and a check on invented competencies (Yan and colleagues; Zou and colleagues).
- GEO tactics guarantee citation, traffic, or a 30–40% visibility gain for stonebyte.bid. Aggarwal and colleagues measured a research benchmark and one engine. Moore and Dunne, a vendor preprint on B2B SaaS pages, find the common on-page checklist fails once the domain is controlled, and domain authority dominates page tweaks.
